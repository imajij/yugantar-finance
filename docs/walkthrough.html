01

Who may touch a rupee

Four roles, one direction of travel, and two places where the chain deliberately bends. This is the spine — every other screen is a view onto it.

Why the chain matters more than it looks. A fest spends real money on somebody else’s behalf — the college’s, a sponsor’s — and is audited afterwards by people who were not there. The question an auditor asks is never “is the total right?” It is “who decided this, and could they have decided it alone?” Three signatures on every rupee, none of which can be the same person, is the answer.

The chain is enforced twice, deliberately. Once in the permission function that every route handler calls before it does anything, and once in the transition table that says which state a record may move to and who may move it. Neither of them is the interface: hiding a button is a courtesy, not a control.

Member

Spends and claims

Photographs receipts, sees their department’s remaining budget, raises tickets. Cannot see another department at all.

Department HOD

Owns a budget’s shape

Splits the allocation into line items, endorses their team’s claims, raises vendor contracts, triages the ticket inbox, runs the department’s channels.

Finance HOD

Owns the money

Sets the fest total, allocates to departments, approves claims and contracts, resolves flags, manages people and settings.

Management

Moves the money

Records every payout with its reference and date. The only role that can — and never for their own claim.

Administrator · a flag, not a role

Oversight without authority

Reads the audit log, the close-out pack, every department’s numbers and every channel. Gains no power to approve or pay anything, even as a Member.

A rule with teeth

The last administrator

Cannot be removed or deactivated, by themselves or anyone else. A fest with none has nobody who can appoint one and no way back to the audit log.

02

The five numbers

Nobody types in how much has been spent. Five figures are derived from the transactions by SQL views at three levels — line item, department, fest — so every screen agrees.

The same five at every level. A line item has them, a department is the sum of its line items, and the fest is the sum of its departments. Because they come from one SQL definition rather than five screens each doing their own arithmetic, the department page and the close-out pack cannot disagree — which is the failure that makes people stop trusting a finance tool.

Above the five sit three more. Reserve is the fest total minus everything allocated — money not yet given to anybody. Cash position is income actually received minus what has been spent. Projected close is income expected minus spent, committed and pending: the number that answers “do we finish in the black?” before the fest has finished.

Where the money moves. An allocation only ever changes by a transfer between a department and the Reserve, with a reason attached. There is no field anybody can type a new allocation into. A department can never be taken below what it has already committed or spent, and Σ allocations can never exceed the fest total — the single hard block in the whole system.

Line item

“Sound · main PA”

The pot a receipt is charged against, with its own five numbers and its supporting quotations.

Department

Tech, Logistics, Cultural…

The sum of its line items, plus the unplanned buffer between what is allocated and what has been broken down.

Fest

The headline

The sum of every department, beside a Reserve that has not been handed to anybody yet.

03

What each person sees on opening it

The dashboard answers one question, and a different one per role: what is waiting on me?

Member · phone, mid-fest

“Claim a receipt”

Their department’s Available as one figure, the line items with what is left in each, and one button that opens the camera. Their own claims and their tickets, nothing else.

Department HOD

“Three to endorse”

The department’s five numbers, the queue of claims waiting on them, line items with utilisation, the ticket inbox, and a control to ask Finance for a bigger allocation.

Finance HOD

“Nine things are waiting on you”

Fest Available derived from every transaction, the approval queue with anything over 36 hours called out, departments closest to their cap, open flags, and today’s activity.

Management

“Pay 4”

The payment queue grouped by payee with bank details, a downloadable payment batch, and the whole fest read-only. Approving is not theirs; paying is.

The one that catches a stall

When a seat is empty

A segregated workflow deadlocks if nobody holds the next role. If claims are approved and there is no active Management account, Committed climbs and Spent never moves — correct arithmetic, stopped workflow. The budget screen says so, with the amount and a link to fix it: fill the seat, rather than move the authority.

Everywhere

Nothing is a dead end

Every refusal explains itself with the numbers in it — “Tech has already committed or spent ₹3,65,800, so its allocation cannot go below that” — and every consequential action states what will happen before it asks you to confirm.

Designed for the phone, not adapted to it. Fest week happens standing up, at night, one-handed. Submitting a receipt is three steps: photograph, then amount and line item with what will be left shown before you commit, then everything else pre-filled from who you are and correctable in place. Every screen is checked at 360, 390, 768, 1024 and 1440 pixels by an automated sweep that fails the build if anything scrolls sideways.

04

Claims and contracts

Two ways money leaves the fest: somebody spent their own and wants it back, or the fest owes a company. Both are state machines, and the interface never invents a state.

SUBMITTED HOD_ENDORSED APPROVED PAID DECLINED ON_HOLD DRAFT

Colour never carries a state on its own — every chip has its label, because fest week happens at night on cheap phone screens.

05

Work, and who can read about it

Money is only half a fest. The other half is one department asking another for forty chairs by four o’clock, and the conversation around it.

Membership is the wall, not a role check. Another department’s head cannot open this one’s channel because they are not in it — not because a check somewhere said no, which is the kind of thing that gets forgotten at one call site out of twelve. Membership follows the department: moving somebody out of Tech takes their Tech channels with them, and deactivating an account removes it from every channel at once.

06

What catches mistakes

Fourteen rules run on every write, and none of them block anything. The point is that somebody looked.

RuleFires whenSeverity
Duplicate receiptThe same file, or the same amount, date and merchant, has been claimed beforeHigh
Outside the windowThe transaction date falls outside the fest’s receipt windowMedium
Round numberA large amount with no paise, which is often an estimate rather than a receiptLow
Over budgetApproving would take the line item or department below zeroHigh
Split claimSeveral claims from one person in a short window that together clear a thresholdMedium
Unusual payeeThe payee is not the submitter, or the UPI ID has never been seenMedium
Advance-heavyA vendor advance is more than half the contractMedium
Invoice mismatchThe invoice does not match what was approvedHigh
Missing quotesA line item at or above the threshold has no supporting documentMedium
Above the lowest quoteA line item is planned higher than the cheapest quotation attached to itLow
Raised by a personAnyone running a department flags a claim, a line item, a department or a person by handtheir choice

Flags never block a decision. Approving over an open High flag is allowed and asks for a note — and that note is what the audit log keeps. A system that stops people working gets worked around; one that makes them explain themselves gets read.

Underneath the rules sit the guarantees that need no judgement. Two people approving the same request at the same moment cannot both succeed, because the row is locked for the length of the transaction. A double-tap on a bad connection cannot pay twice, because every transition and payment carries an idempotency key. A department cannot be taken below what it has already committed. And Σ allocations can never exceed the fest total, which is the one place the system refuses outright rather than flagging.

08

The rules underneath

Ten decisions the whole system is built to keep. Worth a slide because they are what makes the numbers trustworthy a year later, in front of somebody who was not there.

  1. 1One authorization function. Every route asks it before doing anything; the interface asks the same one to decide what to show, and hiding is never the enforcement.
  2. 2State machines are data. No handler branches on a status, so what a button offers is exactly what the rules allow.
  3. 3The five numbers come from SQL views. Never stored, never recomputed in application code, so two screens cannot disagree.
  4. 4Money is integer paise everywhere, formatted in exactly one place. No floating point ever touches a rupee.
  5. 5Every write is one transaction with its audit event inside it. A crash cannot leave money moved with nothing saying why.
  6. 6Segregation of duties lives in the permission function, not in the interface.
  7. 7Soft delete only, and the audit log is append-only — enforced by trigger and by revoked database grants.
  8. 8Human-readable IDs (RB-0001, YG-142) allocated in the creating transaction, so people can say them out loud.
  9. 9Transitions and payments honour an idempotency key — a double-tap on a bad connection cannot pay twice.
  10. 10Every query is scoped by the caller’s fest. No endpoint accepts a fest ID from the client, because a UUID is not a secret.

09

An eight-minute demo

In order, with the point of each step. Sign in as the Finance HOD; keep a phone to hand for step two.

  1. 0:00Dashboard. Read the first line aloud — “nine things are waiting on you”. Point at the big figure and the bar under it: that is the whole product in one shape.
  2. 1:00Claim a receipt on the phone. Camera, amount, line item. Show the line saying what will be left after the claim, and the one at the end: “₹8,400 shows as pending until it is endorsed.”
  3. 2:30Watch Available move. Back on the budget screen, the department’s Available has already dropped. Nobody typed that. This is the moment the derivation lands — do not rush it.
  4. 3:30Endorse, then approve. Two people, two screens. Then try to approve your own claim: the control is not there. That is segregation of duties, not a disabled button.
  5. 4:30Decline something instead. The dialog states what will happen and where the reason goes: the audit log, against your name, uneditable. Then show it in the log.
  6. 5:30Raise a ticket, move it on the board. Ask another department for something. Drag a card — and drag one somewhere it may not go, to show the board refuse out loud.
  7. 6:30Open a channel you are not in. As an administrator. Read the disclosure in the header, then find your own visit already written into the audit log.
  8. 7:30Close-out. Download a department statement. Everything shown in the last eight minutes is in it, and it prints on one page for an HOD to sign.

If there is time for one more thing

Open the flags screen and acknowledge one. Flags never block a decision — approving over an open High flag is allowed and asks for a note. That note is what the audit log keeps, and it is the difference between a system that stops people working and one that makes them explain themselves.

10

Questions people ask

The ones that come up in the room, answered the way they should be answered — including where the answer is “no”.

Can the Finance HOD pay money to themselves?

No, and not by any route. They cannot approve their own claim — it is routed to Management instead — and they cannot record a payment at all, because that authority belongs only to Management. Two different people have to act before a rupee moves.

The same holds in reverse: Management cannot record a payment against their own claim either.

Who can see my bank details and UPI ID?

Finance and Management see them in full, because they are the people who have to make the transfer. Everyone else — including a department head looking at a vendor their own department contracted — sees a masked number ending in the last four digits.

Masking happens before the data leaves the server, not in the browser.

Can an administrator read my direct messages?

Yes. That is a deliberate decision and the honest answer is worth giving plainly rather than burying. What makes it oversight rather than surveillance is that it is disclosed — the channel header tells everyone in it, in plain words — and recorded: opening a channel you are not a member of writes an entry to the append-only audit log with your name on it.

An administrator cannot post without joining first, which puts their name in the member list where everybody can see it.

If you would not want it read, treat it the way you would treat a message in any organisation’s system. This one at least tells you.

What if somebody makes a mistake — can we delete it?

Nothing is ever really deleted. Records are marked deleted and stop appearing; the row stays, and the audit log entry that created it stays. A removed message keeps its place in a conversation and shows as removed, because a gap reads as somebody being edited out.

Correcting is the normal path: a claim can be sent back to be fixed, a line item can be renamed or resized, an allocation can be moved. Every one of those leaves a reason.

What happens on a bad connection at the venue?

Uploads that fail can be retried, and a claim can be saved as a draft and submitted later. Transitions and payments carry an idempotency key, so if the connection drops after the server acted but before the phone heard back, tapping again returns the original result rather than doing it twice.

It is not a fully offline app: the phone needs signal to submit. If the venue is a dead zone, save the draft and send it from the corridor.

How do we know the numbers are right?

Because nobody enters them. Every figure is computed from the transactions by one SQL definition, which is why a department page and the close-out pack cannot disagree. The arithmetic itself is pinned by a test fixture taken straight from the specification — if the views ever stop implementing it, the build fails.

710 automated tests run on every change, and a separate sweep drives all 76 endpoints against a live server to check that permissions, refusals and the money paths behave as written.

Two people approve the same request at the same moment. What happens?

One succeeds and one is told the record changed while they were looking at it. The row is locked for the length of the transaction, so the second approval cannot slip past the first — and the same lock is what stops two allocation transfers both drawing on a Reserve that only covers one.

Somebody leaves the team, or loses their phone.

Deactivate the account from Admin. It ends every signed-in session immediately, kills any outstanding sign-in link, and removes them from every channel. Their history stays exactly where it is — the claims they submitted, the approvals they made, the reasons they wrote.

Nobody has accepted the Management invitation. Is that a problem?

Yes, and the app now says so rather than letting you find out. Approved claims pile up in Committed and Spent never moves, because only Management can record a payment. The budget screen shows the stalled amount with a link to re-send the invitation.

The fix is to fill the seat, not to move the authority — loosening that rule is the thing the whole design exists to avoid.

Can we hand something to the college finance office?

Yes. The close-out pack produces a fest summary, a statement per department with its five numbers and every transaction behind them, the payment batches, and the vendor and income ledgers. CSV and Excel for anyone who wants to work with the figures, and a print layout that an HOD can sign.

Excel carries amounts as real numbers with Indian grouping, so nothing has to be retyped on the other side.

What stops a fake or duplicated receipt?

The same file uploaded twice is caught by its hash. The same amount, date and merchant claimed twice is flagged as a possible duplicate. Claims from one person clustering in a short window are flagged as a possible split. A payee who is not the submitter, or a UPI ID never seen before, is flagged too.

None of it blocks anything — it puts the claim in front of a person with the reason written next to it, which is the only thing that actually catches fraud.

Can we use it again next year?

Yes. Every record belongs to a fest, and every query is scoped by the caller’s fest — no endpoint accepts a fest ID from the client. Next year’s committee gets a new fest, and this year’s stays readable and unchanged for whoever audits it.

Where does the data live, and what does it cost?

A Postgres database and a file bucket for receipts, both on the free tiers of ordinary hosting. Sign-in links and notifications go out over a transactional email service. Running a fest on it costs nothing beyond a domain.

The one thing to plan for is the receipt bucket, which grows with the fest — a few thousand photographs, not a few million.

What is deliberately not built?

Receipt OCR that reads the amount off a photograph, and the flag rules that depend on it. Push and WhatsApp notifications for urgent tickets. A light theme for the app itself — emails and printed exports are already light. And there is no offline mode.

Saying what is missing is part of the demo. A tool that claims everything is the one people stop believing.
